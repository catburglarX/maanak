Who this notice covers

Three groups of people appear in the records this system keeps.

Members of the public who report a problem with a packaged product. Officers who hold an account and carry out inspections. People who appear incidentally in a photograph of a package, a shopfront or a bill, because somebody happened to photograph them along with the evidence.

The third group is the one most privacy notices forget. If you appear in a photograph submitted to this system and you want to know what is held about you, the route is the same as for anyone else and it is set out in section 11.

Who is responsible

Under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, the entity that decides the purpose and means of processing is the Data Fiduciary, and you are the Data Principal.

This deployment names no Data Fiduciary. It runs as a working implementation and no public authority has adopted it, so there is no organisation to name and no published contact channel. That is a real gap and not a formality: it means the rights described in section 11 have no staffed route to reach in this deployment, and the mechanisms that serve them exist in the software without anybody on the other end of them.

An authority deploying this software has to name itself here, publish a contact route, appoint the person who answers questions about processing, and set the retention periods described in section 6. Until that happens, treat this notice as a description of the software's behaviour rather than as a commitment by anyone.

The law this notice is written to

India's data protection regime is mid-transition. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 was enacted on 11 August 2023. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025, and the provisions establishing the Data Protection Board of India came into force on the same date. The substantive obligations on Data Fiduciaries commence on 13 May 2027. The provisions covering consent managers take effect on 13 November 2026.

Until the substantive provisions commence, the operative rules are section 43A and section 72A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 together with the Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011. The DPDP Act repeals all three when it comes fully into force.

This notice is written to the DPDP Act standard ahead of that date because writing to the weaker standard first and upgrading later would mean collecting data now under terms that will not hold.

One point of law matters here and is easy to get wrong. Most obligations under the DPDP Act do not apply where personal data is processed in the interest of preventing, detecting, investigating or prosecuting a contravention of any law in force in India. Legal metrology enforcement plausibly falls inside that carve-out. Whether it does in a particular deployment is a question for the deploying authority and its legal advisers, and this implementation does not assume the answer. It applies the full standard regardless, which is the safer direction to be wrong in.

What is collected

If you report a product

The substance of your report: what the product is, what you saw, where and when you bought it, the prices printed on it, and any photograph or purchase proof you attach. Your name, email address and telephone number, if you choose to give them. The network address and browser identification your device sends when you submit, which are kept to deal with abusive submissions.

A hash of your email address or telephone number is stored separately. The status page compares that hash against what you type in. It exists so that a report reference on its own reveals nothing, and so that your contact details never travel in a URL.

If you hold an officer account

Name, email address and designation, because every decision in this system is attributed to the person who made it. Role and jurisdiction, which determine what you can reach. An Argon2id hash of your password, never the password. Records of sign-in attempts with the address and browser they came from, kept for lockout and investigation. Session records, so that a session can be revoked.

Your name and email address are written into report snapshots, and those snapshots are immutable by database trigger. Closing your account does not remove your name from a report already issued. A report records who decided, and that is what makes it worth anything.

Evidence images

The photograph itself, its SHA-256 hash, its size and dimensions, who uploaded it and when, and the camera make and model where the file records them. Location coordinates are stored only when an officer takes an explicit action to record them.

Coordinates are never read out of photograph metadata. A camera that writes GPS into a file will have that field ignored, so a photograph cannot quietly place an officer somewhere they did not agree to be placed. Derivative images generated for display are stripped of metadata. Originals keep theirs, because editing an original would break the chain of custody that makes the evidence usable.

Technical records

A request identifier that ties a response to a log line and an audit entry. Structured logs, scrubbed of passwords, tokens, secrets and cookies before they are written. Aggregate metrics with no identifiers in them. Rate-limit counters keyed by address or account, which expire with their window.

Contact details are optional

You can report a product without giving your name, email address or telephone number. The form says so before you submit.

The cost of anonymity is that the status page cannot work for you, because there is nothing to check your reference against, and nobody can come back to you for the one extra detail that would have made the report actionable. That is the trade, stated plainly so you can decide.

How long it is kept

No automatic deletion runs in this build. Nothing is erased on a schedule. Records carry a retention state, a retain-until date and a legal-hold flag, and those fields are read when somebody acts on them, but no job acts on them by itself. An authority deploying this software must set the periods and run the deletion. Until it does, assume data stays until somebody removes it.

Some records cannot be deleted by design, and it is better to say which:

What resists deletion, and why
RecordConstraint
Audit events Append-only. A database trigger rejects UPDATE and DELETE. Entries are chained by hash, so removing one breaks verification of every entry after it.
Issued report snapshots Immutable by trigger. A correction produces a new report and withdraws the old one; it never edits the original.
Served notices Frozen once served, for the same reason a served notice cannot be edited on paper.
Anything under legal hold Blocked while the flag is set.

The conflict between a right to erasure and an append-only audit trail is real. This build does not pretend to have solved it. The trail records that an action happened, who took it and when, which is the only basis on which a decision can later be defended or overturned. A deployment that has to erase audit content needs a legal basis for doing so and a written procedure, and there is no endpoint here that will do it.

Who can see it

Officers see records inside their own jurisdiction and no others. The restriction is applied inside the database query, not filtered out of a result afterwards, so a record from another district cannot leak through a list, a count or a search.

Asking for a record outside your jurisdiction returns 404, not 403. A 403 would confirm the record exists, which is itself a disclosure. Controllers see their own jurisdiction and those beneath it. Administrators manage accounts.

Nothing is shared outside the system. There is no analytics provider, no advertising network, no third-party font or script, and no external service that receives any of this data. The site loads only from its own origin, enforced by a default-src 'self' content security policy.

Public endpoints return progress and nothing else. Report verification confirms that a reference exists and that its content is unchanged. It discloses no brand, no premises, no decision and no officer.

How it is protected

Passwords are hashed with Argon2id at 64 MiB of memory, a time cost of 3 and a parallelism of 2. The minimum length is 12 characters. Sign-in is rate-limited to 20 attempts per address in any five-minute window, and that limit fails closed: if the counter store is unreachable, sign-in is refused rather than allowed through.

Sessions live in an HttpOnly cookie that JavaScript cannot read. There are no bearer tokens. Every state-changing request must echo a separate token, so a form submitted from another site is rejected. Access tokens last 15 minutes, refresh tokens 14 days, and a session idles out after 8 hours.

Evidence is held in object storage with server-side encryption and versioning switched on, and reached through links that expire after 5 minutes. Uploads are capped at 20 MB and validated from their actual bytes rather than from the filename or the declared content type.

What is missing matters as much. This deployment terminates no TLS, enforces no multi-factor authentication, scans no upload for malware, and has had no independent penetration test. The security page lists the full set.

What is never collected

  • Plaintext passwords, at any point, anywhere.
  • Plaintext refresh or password-reset tokens. Hashes only.
  • Card or payment details. The system takes no payments.
  • Biometric data. There is no face recognition and no biometric identification of any kind.
  • Location taken from photograph metadata.
  • Caste, religion, health or any other special category, which no field asks for.

Automated processing

Optical character recognition reads the label and proposes what it thinks each declaration says. That output is a machine observation with a state such as located or unreadable. It is not a decision and it is not recorded as one.

An officer separately records whether each reading is confirmed, corrected, rejected or not applicable. Only reviewed values reach the rule engine. A correction is written as a new revision above the original, so both survive and both appear in the report.

No decision affecting anybody is taken by software alone. Findings show the rule version applied, its citation, the values that went in and the arithmetic that produced the result. A named officer records the outcome with a written reason and carries it.

Your rights

The DPDP Act gives a Data Principal the right to access a summary of their personal data and how it has been processed, to have it corrected or erased, to a grievance route, and to nominate somebody to act for them if they die or lose capacity.

Here is what this software can actually do against each, and where it falls short.

Access
If you reported a product, the status page returns its progress using your reference and the contact detail you gave. It returns progress only. It does not return the inspection that followed, because that record contains a third party's business information.
Correction
Officers correct records, and every correction is stored beside the original rather than over it. An issued report cannot be changed. A corrected inspection produces a fresh report and the earlier one is withdrawn, which leaves the change visible instead of silent.
Erasure
Available through the retention fields and manual deletion, subject to the constraints in section 6. A record that an issued report or an open case depends on cannot be removed without destroying the thing it supports.
Grievance
Not available in this deployment. There is no named Data Fiduciary and no published channel, so there is nowhere to send a grievance. A real deployment must publish one. Under the current SPDI Rules a grievance officer has to respond within one month.
Nomination
Not implemented. No field records a nominee.

Once the DPDP Act's substantive provisions commence on 13 May 2027, a Data Principal who is dissatisfied can complain to the Data Protection Board of India. That route exists independently of whatever any operator publishes.

Children

Nothing here is aimed at children, and no field asks for anyone's age. Under the DPDP Act, processing a child's personal data needs verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian, and a child means anybody under 18.

This software performs no age verification. A deployment that expects reports from people under 18 must work out how it will meet that requirement before it opens the form, because the software will not do it.

If there is a breach

Once the DPDP Act's substantive provisions are in force, a Data Fiduciary has to tell the Data Protection Board and every affected Data Principal without delay, and give the Board fuller detail within 72 hours. There is no severity threshold. Every personal data breach is reportable.

Separately, and already in force, directions issued under section 70B of the Information Technology Act, 2000 require certain cyber security incidents to be reported to CERT-In within six hours.

This deployment has no monitoring, no alerting and no incident response process, so it would not reliably detect a breach in the first place. That is stated because a notice claiming a six-hour reporting capability without the detection to support it would be worthless.

Changes to this notice

Each version carries a number and an effective date, shown at the top of this page. The version in force when you submitted a report is stored against that report, so what you agreed to stays on the record even after this page changes.

A change that widens what is collected or what it is used for requires a new version number and fresh consent for anything collected after it. Correcting a typographical error does not.
